Planning & scope

Concept & Audience

Concept Recovery is a calm, guided VR space for the part of rehab that's about rebuilding, not just healing — regaining strength and range of motion lost to injury or surgery, one rep at a time. It brings familiar rehab equipment into a quiet Meta Quest environment, with audio cues that pace your reps and rest intervals as your program progresses. It's not a replacement for a physiotherapist — just steady, structured support for the strength work that happens alone.
Platform & audience Built for Meta Quest, targeting people rebuilding strength after injury or surgery who complete progressive resistance and mobility exercises independently between clinical sessions. Standalone VR was chosen over mobile AR for full environmental control over pacing, calm, and repetition — key to sustaining a low-stimulation, focus-friendly space through a multi-week strength-rebuilding program.
Ethical considerations Recovery is a supplementary motivational and pacing tool, not a medical device. It does not diagnose, prescribe, or correct exercise form — all guidance is limited to rep counting and rest-interval pacing, and users are directed to follow their physiotherapist's actual prescribed program.
Literature review Virtual reality is an increasingly researched tool in physical rehabilitation, not just a novelty add-on. A 2024 review in Current Medicine Research and Practice found VR-based rehabilitation is already used across balance training, muscle strengthening, and gait recovery, with gamification elements — progress tracking, rewards, structured repetition — shown to improve exercise adherence specifically because they make otherwise repetitive routines more engaging.

This adherence problem is well documented outside VR too: a Cochrane review cited in a 2025 knee osteoarthritis physiotherapy study found people are more likely to sustain an exercise program when it feels meaningful and enjoyable rather than purely clinical. Separately, physiotherapist-focused research into VR-supported shoulder rehabilitation found VR can enhance motivation specifically in programs requiring high repetition counts — directly relevant to Recovery's core mechanic of paced, repeated resistance and balance exercises.

Taken together, this supports Recovery's core design bet: that a calm, structured, non-clinical VR space — rather than a gamified or overly playful one — can still meaningfully support adherence for solo rehab work, provided the pacing is handled well. This also informs the technical planning: target platform benchmarks are pulled directly from Meta's own Quest performance guidance (see references, and Asset List) to keep the experience light enough that performance itself doesn't become a barrier to engagement.

Visual direction

Moodboard

Visual direction Reference pulled from real rehab equipment and clinical spaces: resistance bands and rocker balance boards in muted, low-saturation settings — soft grays, warm neutrals, no harsh clinical white. The goal is quiet competence, not a sterile hospital look.
Palette Overcast paper, charcoal ink, tarmac blue-grey, and a single warm amber accent — carried through from the moodboard into the site and, eventually, the VR environment itself.
Audio direction

Sonic Board

Reference sources
  • Pixabay — ambient loops: royalty-free, no attribution required. Source for the low-stimulation background layer.
  • Chosic — calm background music: free with attribution. Reference for a soft, unobtrusive underscore that won't compete with rep/rest audio cues.
  • Freesound.org: source for short interaction sounds — chimes, soft clicks — for the rep-completion and rest-interval cues.
Direction notes Three distinct audio layers, each doing one job: (1) a near-silent ambient bed that keeps the space calm without becoming "meditation app" generic, (2) a soft, short chime confirming each completed rep — no words, just tone, (3) a slightly lower/longer tone marking rest intervals so pacing is audible without a voice narrating every step.

Deployment

Hosting Platform

Decision Hosted on GitHub Pages, serving the site directly from a public repository. Chosen for full control over the custom design system (typography, layout, the departure-board-style header) that a drag-and-drop builder like Wix or Notion can't reproduce, and because it updates in seconds — push a new file, the live site reflects it, no rebuild step.

Self-evaluation

Reflection

Choosing Recovery as my theme came down to genuine interest rather than a calculated decision — once I moved away from the more personal travel-related idea I'd initially considered, Recovery was simply the concept that pulled my attention and felt like something I actually wanted to build, not just complete.

The planning stage was harder than I expected in a different way — not the creative decisions, but understanding exactly what Folio 1 required versus what belonged in later stages. I found myself going back to the assessment brief repeatedly, re-reading the requirements table more than once before the scope actually clicked. It was a good reminder that with a multi-stage assessment like this, checking the source document carefully matters as much as the actual creative work.

Getting the site live on GitHub Pages hit an unexpected snag: the deployment sat "in progress" far longer than usual, and it was genuinely frustrating watching it stall with nothing obviously wrong. I checked the Actions tab and workflow logs to troubleshoot, though nothing new turned up there — GitHub itself was simply slower than usual that time. Having used GitHub since high school, I knew roughly where to look, which made the wait less stressful even without a clear fix, and it deployed successfully shortly after.

Heading into Folio 2, Fusion 360 is the part I'm genuinely looking forward to — I already have experience with it, so building the resistance band anchor and balance board feels achievable. Texturing is the bigger unknown for me; it's a step I haven't done much of before, so it's both exciting and a little intimidating going in.
